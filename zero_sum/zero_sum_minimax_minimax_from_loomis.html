Minimax Theorem by Simplified Loomis Induction

Statement. For every finite matrix game $A : I \times J \to \mathbb{R}$ with $|I|, |J| \ge 1$, the maxmin and minmax values coincide: $$ \lambda_0 = \sup_{x \in \Delta(I)} \min_{j \in J} \sum_i x_i A(i,j) = \inf_{y \in \Delta(J)} \max_{i \in I} \sum_j y_j A(i,j) = \mu_0. $$

This node records the simplified Loomis proof actually used in the Lean formalisation, which works directly on $A$ (no separate $B$) and induces on $n = |I| + |J|$. The general (positive $B$) Loomis theorem is held in Loomis Theorem as future work.

Proof

Strong induction on $n = |I| + |J|$, starting at $n = 2$.

Base case $|I| = |J| = 1$. Both $\lambda_0$ and $\mu_0$ collapse to the unique payoff entry, so they coincide.

Inductive step. Assume the claim for all smaller dimensions. By the weak duality established in Existence of Optimal Mixed Strategies (Loomis Foundations) we already have $\lambda_0 \le \mu_0$; suppose strictly $\lambda_0 < \mu_0$ for contradiction. Pick optimisers $x^\ast, y^\ast$ from the same node.

A short averaging argument shows that strict inequality forces either some column $j_0$ with $\sum_i x^\ast_i A(i, j_0) > \lambda_0$, or some row $i_0$ with $\sum_j y^\ast_j A(i_0, j) < \mu_0$. By symmetry we handle the column case.

The smaller matrix game $A'$ obtained by dropping column $j_0$ has total dimension $n - 1$, so the induction hypothesis gives $\lambda_0(A') = \mu_0(A')$. Extending column strategies on $J' = \{j \ne j_0\}$ to $J$ by putting zero mass on $j_0$ shows $\mu_0(A) \le \mu_0(A')$, hence $\lambda_0(A) < \lambda_0(A')$. Let $x'$ be the inductive optimiser on $A'$. Continuity (from Compactness and Continuity Scaffold for the Loomis Route) lets us pick a strict convex combination $t \cdot x^\ast + (1-t) \cdot x'$ that still beats $\lambda_0(A)$ at every column — including $j_0$ thanks to the open neighborhood around $x^\ast$ where $x^\ast \cdot A(\cdot, j_0) > \lambda_0$. This contradicts the maximality of $\lambda_0(A)$.

The row case is symmetric. Hence $\lambda_0 = \mu_0$.
